Model-Free and Model-Based RL

Learning

Real experience from the environment:

\[(S_t,\,A_t,\,R_{t+1},\,S_{t+1})\]

Model-free RL learns \(v\), \(q\) or \(\pi\) directly from it.

Planning

Simulated experience from a model:

\[(\tilde R,\,\tilde S')\sim\hat{\mathcal M}(s,a)\]

Model-based RL learns \(\hat{\mathcal M}\) from experience, then plans with it.

Planning: computing a value function or policy from a model, without new real experience.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.1

Key line: a simulator supplies consequences; a planner decides which consequences to investigate. Ask learners which arrow touches the real environment. A generative model returns a sampled reward and successor state; it need not enumerate a full transition matrix. A model may come from known rules (games, simulators) or be learned from data. Model-free update rules can run inside a model-based planner, as the next sections show. "Monte-Carlo" describes sampling, not the origin or quality of the model.

Model-Based RL

What Is a Model?

A model \(\hat{\mathcal M}=\langle\hat{\mathcal P},\hat{\mathcal R}\rangle\) approximates the MDP's dynamics: \[\hat{\mathcal P}^a_{ss'}\approx\mathbb P[S_{t+1}=s'\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]\] \[\hat{\mathcal R}^a_s\approx\mathbb E[R_{t+1}\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]\]

  • learning \(s,a\to r\) is a regression problem
  • learning \(s,a\to s'\) is a density estimation problem
  • two sources of error: the model, then the planned values

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.1

This is the course's Lecture 1 model, now estimated. We assume the state and action spaces are known and treat model learning as supervised learning from the pairs (S_t, A_t) → (R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}). Examples: table lookup, linear expectation or linear Gaussian models, Gaussian processes, neural networks. Advantages: supervised learning methods apply, and uncertainty about the model can be reasoned about explicitly. Disadvantage: the planned value inherits the model's error as well as its own approximation error.

Table Lookup Model

Count visits \(N(s,a)\) and average what followed:

\[\hat{\mathcal P}^a_{ss'}=\frac{1}{N(s,a)}\sum_{t}\mathbf 1(S_t,A_t,S_{t+1}=s,a,s')\] \[\hat{\mathcal R}^a_s=\frac{1}{N(s,a)}\sum_{t}\mathbf 1(S_t,A_t=s,a)\,R_{t+1}\]
  • observed frequencies and mean rewards: maximum-likelihood estimates
  • alternative: store every tuple \((S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})\) and sample one that matches \((s,a)\)

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.1

The sums run over all recorded time steps. Before (s, a) has been tried, N(s, a) = 0 and the estimate is undefined, not zero. The stored-tuple version is the sample model used by Dyna-Q: it reproduces the empirical distribution without ever writing down the probabilities. Worked example: if (s, a) was tried 20 times and 13 tries led to a success state with reward 1 (the rest to failure with reward 0), then N(s, a) = 20, the estimated success probability is 13/20 = 0.65 and the estimated reward is also 0.65. More samples reduce uncertainty in the statistical sense, but a single new observation can move the estimate away from the truth.

Planning with a Model

Solve the approximate MDP \(\langle\mathcal S,\mathcal A,\hat{\mathcal P},\hat{\mathcal R}\rangle\) with any planner: value iteration, policy iteration, tree search. Each uses backups:

\[Q(s,a)=\hat{\mathcal R}^a_s+\gamma\sum_{s'}\hat{\mathcal P}^a_{ss'}V(s')\]

Example: \(\hat{\mathcal R}^a_s=-0.1\); the successor is worth \(V=1\) with \(\hat{\mathcal P}=0.6\), otherwise \(V=0\); \(\gamma=0.9\). \[Q(s,a)=-0.1+0.9\,(0.6\times1+0.4\times0)=0.44\] Break-even: \(Q(s,a)=0\) when \(\hat{\mathcal P}=0.1/\gamma\approx0.11\).

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.1

One expected backup with supplied successor values (they are not learned here). With γ = 0.9: Q = −0.1 + 0.9 × 0.6 = 0.44, and Q = 0 when the success probability is 0.1/0.9 ≈ 0.111; at γ = 0 only the immediate reward −0.1 remains, so no probability breaks even. The two outcomes are stochastic transitions, so they are averaged: the planner cannot choose the successful one. Distinguish uncertainty about which successor occurs (the probability) from uncertainty about whether the model's probability is right (the inaccurate-model slide). The next slide replaces the sum over successors by samples.

Sample-Based Planning

Use the model only to generate samples: \[S_{t+1}\sim\hat{\mathcal P}(\,\cdot\mid S_t,A_t),\qquad R_{t+1}=\hat{\mathcal R}(S_t,A_t)\] then apply model-free RL to the simulated experience.

  • Monte-Carlo control, Sarsa, Q-learning
  • samples concentrate on likely outcomes
  • often more efficient than full-width backups
  • needs only a generative model

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.1

Full-width planning (value iteration) sums over every successor. Sample-based planning replaces the sum by samples, exactly as Monte-Carlo and TD learning replace it in model-free prediction, so the whole model-free toolbox from Lectures 4–5 becomes a planning method. The efficiency claim is a tendency, not a theorem: rare but important outcomes may be sampled late. The next slide is Silver's AB example.

Interactive: Planning with an Inaccurate Model

Risky: pays 2 on success, 0 on failure; real success probability 0.6. Safe: always pays 1.3. Whisker: model estimate ± 2 standard errors after n simulations.

A planner can do no better than the optimal policy of the approximate MDP.

Raise n from 10 to 1000. Which error shrinks, and which does not? Which model probabilities choose wrongly?

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.3

This is an analytical calculator, not a Monte-Carlo run. A simulated risky reward under the model is 2 × Bernoulli(p), where p is the model's success probability, so the mean of n independent simulations has expectation 2p and standard error SE = 2√(p(1−p)/n); the black whisker is the band 2p ± 2 SE, cut to the possible range 0–2. The true risky value is 1.2, below the safe 1.3. At the default p = 0.8 the model predicts 1.60, a bias of +0.40 at every n. The ±2 SE band is 1.09–2.00 at n = 10 (it contains 1.30, so a 10-sample estimate could by chance prefer safe), 1.44–1.76 at n = 100 and 1.55–1.65 at n = 1000: more simulations shrink the sampling error as 1/√n and make the wrong preference more certain, but leave the bias unchanged. Any model with p > 0.65 (0.70 and above on the slider) prefers the wrong action; p = 0.65 ties; p ≤ 0.6 chooses safe, though p < 0.6 is also biased. The standard error excludes parameter uncertainty and misspecification. Silver's two remedies: fall back on model-free RL when the model is wrong, or reason explicitly about model uncertainty.

Integrated Architectures

Dyna Architecture

Two kinds of experience

Real, from the environment:

\[S'\sim\mathcal P^a_{ss'},\quad R=\mathcal R^a_s\]

Simulated, from the model:

\[S'\sim\hat{\mathcal P}^a_{ss'},\quad R=\hat{\mathcal R}^a_s\]

Each real step

  1. act and observe a transition
  2. direct RL: update values
  3. model learning: update \(\hat{\mathcal M}\)
  4. planning: update values again from simulated transitions

Dyna: learn a model from real experience; learn and plan values from both real and simulated experience.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.2; David Silver, Lecture 8

Model-free RL uses only real experience; pure sample-based planning uses only simulated experience. Dyna uses both. The same value update is applied to both kinds of transition, so planning gives each real transition extra work. Reusing a wrong model can reinforce a wrong answer, which is why direct RL from real data stays in the loop.

Dyna-Q Algorithm

For each real step from state \(S\):

  1. \(A\leftarrow\varepsilon\)-greedy\((S,Q)\); take \(A\), observe \(R,S'\)
  2. \(Q(S,A)\leftarrow Q(S,A)+\alpha\big[R+\gamma\max_a Q(S',a)-Q(S,A)\big]\)
  3. \(\text{Model}(S,A)\leftarrow R,S'\) (deterministic world)
  4. repeat \(n\) times: pick an observed \((S,A)\), set \(R,S'\leftarrow\text{Model}(S,A)\), apply update 2
  5. \(S\leftarrow S'\)
\(n\) planning updates per real step: computation is traded for real experience.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.2; David Silver, Lecture 8

Tabular Dyna-Q: step 2 is direct RL (Q-learning), step 3 is model learning, step 4 is planning. Planning samples state–action pairs uniformly from those already observed; with n = 0 this is plain Q-learning. Uniform replay wastes updates on pairs whose successor values have not changed: on a stored path A → B → C → end with rewards 0, 0, 1, γ = 0.9 and α = 1, replaying C, B, A backwards reaches the exact values 1, 0.9, 0.81 in three backups, while forward sweeps A, B, C need seven. Prioritised sweeping (Sutton and Barto §8.4) backs up the predecessors of recently changed values first. In a changing world the model becomes stale: Sutton and Barto's Dyna-Q+ adds a bonus κ√τ to simulated rewards, where τ is the time since the pair was last tried in reality, to encourage re-testing. The next slide runs this algorithm.

Simulation-Based Search

Forward Search

  • build a search tree rooted at the current state \(s_t\)
  • look ahead with the model
  • solve only the sub-MDP starting now

A full tree with branching factor \(b\) and depth \(d\) has \[1+b+\dots+b^d=\frac{b^{d+1}-1}{b-1}\] nodes: 1,111,111 for \(b=10,\ d=6\).

Sampling helps when exploring every branch is unaffordable.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)

Global planning (Dyna, value iteration) improves values across many states for future decisions. Forward search spends computation on the decision in front of the agent. The node count assumes a regular tree with distinct nodes and no merging of transpositions. A search can reuse its subtree after the real agent acts. A generative model suffices for sampling even when enumerating every transition probability is impractical. Search allocates finite computation; its output can still be wrong because of sampling, depth limits or model error.

Simulation-Based Search

Simulate \(K\) episodes from now with the model, then apply model-free RL to them: \[\{s_t,A^k_t,R^k_{t+1},S^k_{t+1},\dots,S^k_T\}_{k=1}^{K}\sim\hat{\mathcal M}\]

Model-free RLSearch methodBacks up
Monte-Carlo controlMonte-Carlo searchcomplete simulated returns
SarsaTD searchbootstrapped targets

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8

Simulated episodes all start from the current real state s_t. As in model-free learning, bootstrapping trades variance for bias: TD search is often more efficient than Monte-Carlo search, especially when many simulated paths reach the same states. Silver's Dyna-2 combines a long-term memory learned from real experience with a short-term memory updated by TD search during each decision. TD search and Dyna-2 are follow-up reading; the rest of this lecture develops Monte-Carlo search.

Simple Monte-Carlo Search

Given a model \(\hat{\mathcal M}\) and a fixed simulation policy \(\pi\):

  1. for each action \(a\), simulate \(K\) episodes from \(s_t\)
  2. evaluate actions by mean return: \[Q(s_t,a)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}G^k_t\;\to\;q_\pi(s_t,a)\]
  3. take the real action \(a_t=\arg\max_a Q(s_t,a)\)
This estimates \(q_\pi\) for the rollout policy, not \(q_*\): a bad continuation can make a good first move look bad.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)

Also called flat Monte-Carlo or rollout search. The convergence is in probability as K grows, to the value of the rollout policy π. If the rollout policy blunders after a promising first move, the search estimates the return of that blundering continuation: a meaningful policy value, but it may rank actions differently from optimal play. The result is still a one-step improvement over π. MCTS fixes this by letting the continuation improve inside a growing tree; the interactive slide after the tree trace compares the two on the same problem.

Monte-Carlo Tree Search

Monte-Carlo Tree Search

Simulate episodes from \(s_t\), store visited states and actions in a tree, and evaluate each edge by its mean return: \[Q(s,a)=\frac{1}{N(s,a)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u=t}^{T}\mathbf 1(S^k_u,A^k_u=s,a)\,G^k_u\]

Tree policy (improves)

Inside the tree: choose by \(Q\) plus an exploration bonus.

Rollout policy (fixed)

Beyond the tree: e.g. uniformly random actions.

Monte-Carlo control on simulated experience: with UCT, \(Q(s_t,a)\to q_*(s_t,a)\) as simulations grow.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)

N(s, a) counts the simulations that passed through edge (s, a); G is the return from that point in simulation k. Each simulation evaluates states by Monte-Carlo and then improves the tree policy, so this is Monte-Carlo control applied to simulated experience. The convergence statement is Kocsis and Szepesvári's consistency result for UCT under finite-horizon (or truncated discounted) MDPs with bounded rewards; it says nothing about a finite budget. Because the tree policy changes, the root average is not an unbiased sample mean of a fixed q.

Four Phases of a Simulation

  1. Select: follow the tree policy
  2. Expand: store one untried child
  3. Evaluate: roll out, or estimate the leaf
  4. Back up: update the path

Each stored edge keeps

\[N(s,a),\quad W(s,a)\] \[Q(s,a)=\frac{W(s,a)}{N(s,a)}\]

visits, total return and mean return.

Repeat within the budget, then commit by a stated rule, e.g. most visits: \[a_t=\arg\max_a N(s_t,a)\]

An action can deserve another simulation without deserving the real move.

Sources: Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006); Silver et al. (2017), AlphaZero

Implementations package expansion and evaluation differently, but these roles remain. The demos expand one node per simulation. Selection follows stored nodes only; rollouts visit model states without storing them, and a completed simulation updates N and W only on the selected and expanded path. Root decision rules differ: most visits (used here), highest mean Q (Silver's slide), or sampling from visit counts during training. Do not apply the exploration bonus to the real commitment. Compare methods under equal model-evaluation or wall-clock budgets, not equal numbers of differently costly iterations.

Interactive: UCT Selection

\[Q(s,a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)}}\]

A: \(Q=0.55\) after 12 visits. B: \(Q=0.45\). \(N(s)\) is the sum of child visits.

At c = 1, how many visits does B need before A is selected again?

Sources: Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)

UCT applies the UCB1 bandit rule at every tree node. N(s) is the parent's visit count and N(s, a) the action's; unvisited legal actions are tried first, so there is no division by zero. Texts absorb factors such as √2 into c, and c must match the reward scale. At the defaults A ≈ 0.55 + 0.475 = 1.025 and B ≈ 0.45 + 0.950 = 1.400, so B is selected despite its lower mean. Answer to the prompt: at c = 1, B needs 9 visits (A 1.054 against B 1.032); at 8 visits B still wins (1.050 against 1.062). At c = 0 the scores are the means, so A always wins; larger c needs more visits to B (11 at c = 3), smaller c fewer (2 at c = 0.1). The bonus falls with the action's own visits and rises slowly with the parent's. Here N(s) = 12 + N(s, B), the sum of child visits, as at the root of the trace; deeper in a tree N(s) is the node's own visit count, which also counts the simulation that expanded it. This calculator holds the means fixed; a real search updates both, and subtree returns are not stationary bandit samples.

Backing Up a Return

A simulation returns \(G\). Each edge on the stored path updates

\[\begin{aligned}N(s,a)&\leftarrow N(s,a)+1\\ W(s,a)&\leftarrow W(s,a)+G\\ Q(s,a)&\leftarrow W(s,a)/N(s,a)\end{aligned}\]

Incremental form: \(Q\leftarrow Q+\frac{G-Q}{N}\), with the updated \(N\).

Example

An edge has \(N=3\), \(W=1.5\), \(Q=0.5\). A new simulation returns \(G=0.8\):

\[N=4,\quad W=2.3,\quad Q=\frac{2.3}{4}=0.575\]

Check: \(0.5+\frac{0.8-0.5}{4}=0.575\).

The new mean lies between the old mean and \(G\): MCTS averages returns; it does not keep the best one.

Sources: Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006); David Silver, Lecture 8

With the same edge, G = 0 gives Q = 1.5/4 = 0.375 and G = 1 gives 2.5/4 = 0.625: one return moves Q a quarter of the way towards G, never beyond it. A max backup would jump to 0.8 and keep the luckiest rollout, which overestimates noisy actions. The incremental form uses the updated N and is algebraically identical to W/N. With intermediate rewards, each edge needs its own return-to-go, accumulated backwards along the path as G ← R + γG; copying one terminal reward along the path is valid only under the single-agent, terminal-reward, γ = 1 convention of the tree trace on the next slide. Opponent perspectives are handled in the last section.

Advantages of MCTS

  • highly selective, best-first search
  • evaluates states dynamically, from the current state, unlike DP over all states
  • sampling breaks the curse of dimensionality
  • works for black-box models: needs only samples
  • computationally efficient, anytime and parallelisable
Case study: computer Go, where hand-written position evaluation is hard but simulated games are cheap.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 8; Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)

Silver's lecture uses Go as the case study: the value of a position is estimated by the mean outcome of simulated games, and the search tree grows asymmetrically towards promising moves. Anytime means the search can return its current answer when interrupted; it does not mean each extra sample monotonically improves that answer. Parallel MCTS needs care because simultaneous simulations see the same stale statistics. These are qualitative advantages; this deck makes no performance claim.

Using Search Well

Where the Rollout Stops

A rollout cut off after \(h\) steps must evaluate the state where it stops:

\[G=\sum_{k=1}^{h}\gamma^{k-1}R_{t+k}+\gamma^{h}\hat v(S_{t+h})\]

Example (\(\gamma=0.9\))

  • short route: 0.45 after one step
  • long route: 1 after six steps, so \(G=\gamma^{5}\approx0.59\)
  • the long route is better

Cut off at \(h=3\)

  • \(\hat v=0\): long route \(\approx 0\); search picks short
  • \(\hat v=\gamma^{2}=0.81\) (exact): \(0.9^{3}\times0.81\approx0.59\); picks long
A computational cutoff is not a terminal state: leaving out \(\hat v\) assumes it is 0.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), Chapter 8; David Silver, Lecture 8

The long route's reward arrives as R_{t+6}, so its exact return is γ⁵ (0.590 at γ = 0.9), not γ⁶. With h < 6 the estimate is γ^h v̂(S_{t+h}), because the first h rewards are 0. At h = 3 and γ = 0.9 the exact remaining value is γ² = 0.81, and inserting it gives γ³ × 0.81 = 0.59049, the exact return; any v̂ above 0.45/0.729 ≈ 0.617 makes the search pick the long route. With v̂ = 0, an omitted bootstrap is a hidden zero-value assumption. At h = 6 the episode terminates and no v̂ is needed. At γ = 0.5 the short route really is better (0.5⁵ ≈ 0.031), so a deeper search need not choose the long route; the break-even discount is 0.45^(1/5) ≈ 0.852. In MCTS the leaf evaluation may be a rollout, a truncated rollout plus v̂, or v̂ alone (a value network, as on the Policy Priors and Value Networks slide).

Chance and Opponent Nodes

Two outcomes, worth +1 and −1 to the root player. Who picks the branch decides the value:

NodeRuleValue to the root player
our decisionmaximise\(\max(+1,-1)=+1\)
chance, \(p(+1)=0.75\)average\(0.75(+1)+0.25(-1)=0.5\)
zero-sum opponentminimise\(\min(+1,-1)=-1\)
Chance nodes sample outcomes; opponents choose them. Treating an opponent as chance changes the problem.

Sources: Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006); David Silver, Lecture 10: Classic Games

Three node types: our decision (maximise), chance (average under the probability law: 2p − 1 for a general p, 0.5 at p = 0.75) and an opponent's decision. A random-opponent model gives an expectation; a perfect-information zero-sum adversary chooses the best response, −1 for every p, so no p makes the opponent's branch worth more than −1. In MCTS, opponent nodes either maximise values stored from the opponent's perspective, or minimise root-perspective values with a matching exploration bonus. Never maximise root-player Q at both. With alternating player-to-move values, zero-sum returns change sign during backup; with a fixed root perspective, the stored sign stays and the selection rule changes. Chance nodes sample outcomes; they never choose optimistic ones.

Quick Check: Whose Value?

  • all stored returns are measured for the root player
  • at an opponent node, one move gives +0.8 and the other −0.4 to the root player
  • ignore exploration

Which move should a rational zero-sum opponent choose?

Answer: −0.4. Make the convention explicit before writing any code. A different answer would be right if the numbers were measured for the opponent, but they are not. This is not an instruction to negate every edge: stochastic transitions, same-player turns and intermediate rewards need a convention that matches the game or MDP. Ask which assumption changed between the trace and this slide.

Policy Priors and Value Networks

\[(\mathbf p,\,v)=f_\theta(s)\]
  • prior \(P(s,a)\): where to search first
  • value \(v(s)\): evaluates a leaf instead of a rollout

AlphaZero: known game rules + MCTS + one network.

Search improves the move distribution; self-play outcomes train the next network.

Not implemented in the toy demos.

Sources: Silver et al. (2017), AlphaZero, algorithm description

The conceptual addition is two learned outputs, not a larger rollout budget. The policy head supplies prior move probabilities; the value head replaces random rollouts at the leaf. Search produces visit-count targets for the policy and games produce outcome targets for the value. The network does not replace the known legal-move and transition rules in AlphaZero. Training, exploration noise, terminal handling, player perspective, batching and evaluation protocol all matter. This slide states mechanisms and does not reproduce benchmark claims.

Prior-Guided Selection

AlphaZero-style (PUCT) selection adds a prior to the bonus:

\[Q(s,a)+c\,P(s,a)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1+N(s,a)}\]

Example: \(N(s)=12\), \(c=2\):

Action\(Q\)\(N(s,a)\)\(P(s,a)\)Score
A0.2100.7\(0.2+2\times0.7\times\sqrt{12}/11\approx0.64\)
B0.620.3\(0.6+2\times0.3\times\sqrt{12}/3\approx1.29\)
B is simulated next. Only a prior \(P(s,A)\gtrsim0.92\) would send the search back to A.

Sources: Silver et al. (2017), AlphaZero, algorithm description

An illustrative PUCT-style score, not a replication of AlphaZero search or its hyperparameter schedule. The contrast with UCT is the prior P(s, a) and the 1 + N(s, a) denominator, which keeps the score finite for unvisited actions. Worked: √12 ≈ 3.464; A's bonus is 2 × 0.7 × 3.464 / 11 ≈ 0.441, so 0.641; B's is 2 × 0.3 × 3.464 / 3 ≈ 0.693, so 1.293. With P(s, B) = 1 − P(s, A), the scores cross at P(s, A) ≈ 0.922. With P(s, A) = 1, c above about 1.27 makes A win. A prior of exactly zero can suppress an action under this score; practical systems add exploration deliberately (e.g. root noise). Values and counts are held fixed to isolate the score.

Before Trusting a Search Result

Model and information

  • Does the model reproduce the relevant consequences?
  • Are chance and opponent nodes distinct?
  • Could the search see hidden information?

Computation and evaluation

  • Was a cutoff mistaken for termination?
  • Were values backed up in one clear convention?
  • Does the choice survive other seeds and budgets?
A consistent planner can still solve the wrong decision problem.

Sources: Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006); David Silver, Lecture 8; Silver et al. (2017)

The UCT paper gives consistency results under particular finite-horizon or discounted MDP and bounded-reward assumptions, not a finite-budget guarantee for any learned-model or neural implementation. Hidden-information games need information-state reasoning; searching with the opponent's actual hidden cards is not valid play. Distribution shift in learned models and rare outcomes matter especially outside games. Numerical correctness, model validity and application performance are separate checks.

Summary

\[\text{experience}\;\to\;\text{model}\;\to\;\text{simulations}\;\to\;\text{better-informed action}\]
  • model-based RL: learn \(\hat{\mathcal P},\hat{\mathcal R}\), then plan with samples
  • Dyna: learn and plan from real and simulated experience
  • MCTS: select, expand, evaluate, back up; explore while searching, commit by a stated rule
  • every estimate depends on the model, rollout policy, horizon and value perspective
