Recurring question: is the information from this action worth its immediate cost?

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), Chapters 2 and 8

Check prerequisite recall: what does ε-greedy do in Lecture 5, and what is the incremental form of a sample mean? The tension of the lecture is between immediate reward and gathering information that may improve later decisions. Exploration is not useful in itself: it is useful when what is learnt changes a future choice enough to justify its cost. We begin with independent Bernoulli arms, so uncertainty and action selection can be inspected directly, and later add context, change over time and state transitions. Rewards are dimensionless successes; nothing here is a recommendation to deploy an exploration rule in a particular real service.

Exploration and Exploitation

Online decision-making involves a fundamental choice:

  • Exploitation: make the best decision given current information
  • Exploration: gather more information
  • the best long-term strategy may involve short-term sacrifices
  • gather enough information to make the best overall decisions
ExampleExploitExplore
Restaurantyour favouritea new one
Online advertsthe most successfula different one
Oil drillingthe best known sitea new site
Game playingthe best known movean experimental move

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation

Silver's five principles organise the rest of the lecture: naive exploration (add noise to a greedy policy, e.g. ε-greedy), optimistic initialisation (assume the best until proven otherwise), optimism in the face of uncertainty (prefer actions with uncertain values, e.g. UCB), probability matching (select actions according to the probability that they are best, e.g. Thompson sampling) and information state search (look ahead, including the value of information). Ask for one more example from the class and for its "cost" of exploring: a bad meal, a lost click, a dry well. In each example, exploring only pays if there are enough future decisions to use what is learnt.

Multi-Armed Bandits

The Multi-Armed Bandit

A multi-armed bandit has \(m\) actions (“arms”), each with an unknown reward distribution. At each step \(t\) the agent selects an action \(A_t\) and receives a reward \(R_t\) from that arm.

\[q_*(a)=\mathbb{E}[R_t\mid A_t=a],\qquad v_*=\max_a q_*(a)\]
  • goal: maximise the expected total reward \(\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_t\big]\)
  • stationary: each arm's distribution is fixed; draws are independent
  • no state: an action changes what we know, not what happens next

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.1; Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

Notation follows Sutton and Barto: q*(a) is the true mean reward of arm a (Silver writes Q(a)), v* the best mean, T the number of available steps. The true means are properties of the environment; estimates are properties of the learner. In the standard stationary stochastic setting, pulling an arm does not change any arm's reward distribution. This separates a bandit from an MDP, where actions influence which states are reached. A bandit is still sequential in what the learner knows: past rewards change future choices. Bernoulli rewards are a convenient special case whose mean is the success probability; bounded, Gaussian and contextual models need their own assumptions. A bandit can be seen as an MDP with a single state.

Estimating Action Values

Incremental mean

After selecting \(A_t\) and observing \(R_t\):

\[N(A_t)\leftarrow N(A_t)+1\] \[Q(A_t)\leftarrow Q(A_t)+\frac{1}{N(A_t)}\big(R_t-Q(A_t)\big)\]

Rewards 1, 0, 1 from one arm

\[\begin{aligned}Q&=1\\ Q&=1+\tfrac{1}{2}(0-1)=0.5\\ Q&=0.5+\tfrac{1}{3}(1-0.5)=\tfrac{2}{3}\end{aligned}\]

\(N_t(a)\) and \(Q_t(a)\): the count and sample mean of arm \(a\) after \(t\) steps.

Only the chosen arm's estimate changes: the other arms' rewards were not observed.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.4; David Silver, Lecture 9

This is the incremental mean of Lecture 4 applied to one arm. Derive it by separating the newest reward from the sum of the earlier ones. Increment the count before dividing. The sample mean weights every observation equally and needs only a count and an estimate per arm. When the initial count is zero, the initial value of Q does not matter after the first real observation, because the first step size is 1. If an optimistic initial value is meant to persist as evidence, a pseudocount or a constant step size changes the update; the Optimistic Initialisation slide uses a pseudocount. Independent arm models update only the chosen arm; structured bandits can share information across arms, but that needs an explicit model.

Regret

The gap of action \(a\) is \(\Delta_a=v_*-q_*(a)\). The total regret after \(t\) steps is \[L_t=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\Delta_{A_\tau}=\sum_a N_t(a)\,\Delta_a\]

  • expected regret: \(\mathbb{E}[L_t]=\sum_a\mathbb{E}[N_t(a)]\,\Delta_a\)
  • maximising expected reward \(\equiv\) minimising expected regret
  • a good algorithm keeps counts small for large gaps, but the gaps are unknown

Example: means \((0.35,\ 0.50,\ 0.65)\) give \(\Delta=(0.30,\ 0.15,\ 0)\); pulls \((20,\ 30,\ 50)\) give \(L_{100}=20\times0.30+30\times0.15=10.5\).

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

The example uses the three arms of the interactive slides. Its regret, 10.5 expected successes forgone in 100 pulls (an average gap of 0.105 per pull), does not depend on the outcomes: a failure on C adds nothing. The allocations (35, 0, 65) and (5, 60, 35) also give 10.5, with very different numbers of best-arm pulls. One pull of A costs as much as two pulls of B, so a good algorithm should spend its exploration where gaps are small, which it cannot do without estimating them. Gaps compare true means, not individual outcomes. A failure on the best arm adds zero regret, and a lucky success on a worse arm still adds its positive gap. L_t as written is the pathwise pseudo-regret: a sum of gaps along the actions actually taken. Silver defines total regret directly as the expectation, which is E[L_t] here: t·v* minus the expected total reward. Neither is the realised reward shortfall t·v* − ΣR, which is noisy and can be negative through luck. A simulation can compute gaps because it knows the means; a real learner cannot observe its own regret.

Quick Check: Greedy Lock-In

Greedy selects the best current estimate:

\[A_{t+1}=\operatorname*{argmax}_a\,Q_t(a)\]

After one pull each, A returned 1 and B returned 0, so \(Q(A)=1\) and \(Q(B)=0\).

Why can continuing greedily be a problem?

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.2

Answer: B may never be tried again. The question is about a possible failure, not a claim that greedy fails on every run. With Bernoulli rewards, A can keep a positive sample mean while B stays at zero and is never revisited. This event has positive probability even when B is better, which gives linear expected regret. Random tie-breaking does not help once the ordering is strict. Ask whether forcing one pull of each arm is enough: it avoids never-initialised estimates but does not prevent a noisy early misranking, as this example shows.

\(\varepsilon\)-Greedy

  • with probability \(1-\varepsilon\): the greedy action \(\operatorname{argmax}_a Q_t(a)\)
  • with probability \(\varepsilon\): an action chosen uniformly from all \(m\)
\[\pi(a)=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/m & \text{if } a=\operatorname{argmax}_{a'}Q_t(a')\\ \varepsilon/m & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

Example: \(m=3\) and \(\varepsilon=0.3\) give probabilities \(0.8,\ 0.1,\ 0.1\).

An exploratory draw can also select the greedy arm.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.2

This is the same rule as in Lecture 5, without a state. State the convention, because some implementations explore only among the non-greedy actions, which gives different probabilities; this deck samples uniformly from all arms, including the current winner. Ties in the greedy choice are broken uniformly at random in the simulations. The worked example: 1 − 0.3 + 0.3/3 = 0.8 for the greedy arm and 0.1 for each other arm. A fixed ε keeps every arm covered, but it keeps spending trials on arms already known to be poor, and it treats a well-measured arm and a barely tried arm alike. Those two weaknesses motivate the confidence-based and posterior-based methods later; they do not make ε-greedy useless.

Optimistic Initialisation

  • initialise every estimate high, then act greedily
  • each disappointment lowers the tried arm below the untried ones

\(Q_0\) is held as one pseudocount, never counted as a pull:

\[Q_t(a)=\frac{Q_0+\text{successes}}{1+N_t(a)}\]

The three arms, greedy, seed 901:

\(Q_0\)First pullsThen\(N_{30}(a)\)
1A→1, C→1, A→0\(Q(A)=\tfrac{1+1}{1+2}=\tfrac23<1\): try B, still at 1(3, 5, 22)
0A→1\(Q(A)=\tfrac{0+1}{1+1}=\tfrac12>0\): A for ever(30, 0, 0)
Optimism is an early push, not a lasting measure of uncertainty.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.6; David Silver, Lecture 9

Silver's second principle: assume the best until proven otherwise. Every arm looks worth trying until its own rewards pull its estimate down. The pseudocount makes Q₀ act as one unit of prior evidence; with an initial count of zero the first real pull would overwrite Q₀, because the first step size is 1. The worked run uses the three arms (0.35, 0.50, 0.65) with the reward streams of Try the Arms (seed 901) and random tie-breaking from a fixed stream. With Q₀ = 1 a success leaves an estimate at 1 and a failure lowers it: after A→1, C→1, A→0, Q(A) = 2/3 while B and C are still at 1, so B is tried next. All three arms are tried within four pulls; after 30 pulls N = (3, 5, 22) and the regret is 1.65. With Q₀ = 0 the first pull (a tie-break) is A, which succeeds: Q(A) = 1/2 > 0 while B and C stay at exactly 0, so greedy pulls A for ever, N = (30, 0, 0), regret 9.0 after 30 pulls and 90 after 300. Optimism is an initial push, not a persistent measure of uncertainty: with other reward streams it can still lock onto a suboptimal arm, and it does not help if the means later change. Sutton and Barto show the same effect with Q₁ = 5 on their 10-armed testbed.

Linear or Sublinear Regret

Fixed \(\varepsilon>0\)

\[\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{A_t}\big]\ \ge\ \frac{\varepsilon}{m}\sum_a\Delta_a\]

A constant cost at every step: linear regret. Greedy can lock on, so its regret is also linear.

Decaying \(\varepsilon_t\)

  • too fast: early mistakes persist
  • too slow: many avoidable bad pulls
\[\varepsilon_t=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{c\,m}{d^2\,t}\Big\}\]

is logarithmic, but needs the smallest gap \(d\) in advance.

Sublinear regret means \(\mathbb{E}[L_t]/t\to 0\). Total regret can still grow without bound.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer (2002), Theorem 3

The bound follows from the exploration branch alone: with probability ε the action is uniform, so each arm is chosen with probability at least ε/m and contributes its non-negative gap. The exploitation branch can only add loss. So a fixed positive ε gives regret linear in t whenever at least one gap is positive; if all means are equal every gap is zero and the statement is empty. Auer et al. show that the schedule ε_t = min{1, cm/(d²t)} with c > 5 and d no larger than the smallest positive gap has logarithmic regret, but it needs d in advance. A generic 1/t schedule is not a proof of logarithmic regret. Average regret can tend to zero while cumulative regret grows without bound.

Upper Confidence Bounds

Lower Bound

  • hard problems have similar-looking arms with different means
  • similarity: the gap \(\Delta_a\) and the divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{R}^a\,\|\,\mathcal{R}^{a^*})\)

Theorem (Lai and Robbins). For any consistent algorithm, total regret grows at least logarithmically: \[\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[L_t]}{\ln t}\ \ge\ \sum_{a:\Delta_a>0}\frac{\Delta_a}{\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{R}^a\,\|\,\mathcal{R}^{a^*}\big)}\]

Goal: logarithmic regret on every bandit, without knowing the gaps.

Sources: Lai & Robbins (1985); David Silver, Lecture 9; Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

R^a is the reward distribution of arm a and a* an optimal arm. KL is the Kullback–Leibler divergence: it is small when the two distributions are hard to tell apart from samples. "Consistent" means that on every instance of the model class (for example all Bernoulli bandits) the expected regret grows more slowly than any power t^p, p > 0; this rules out an algorithm that simply guesses one arm and is lucky on some instances. Silver writes the limit without the liminf and with total regret as an expectation; the content is the same. For Bernoulli arms Pinsker's inequality gives KL ≥ 2Δ², so each term is at most 1/(2Δ_a): the lower bound is smaller than UCB1's upper bound on the next slides, as it must be. The theorem fixes the order, log t, not the constant.

Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty

The more uncertain an action value, the more important it is to explore that action: it could be the best.

Estimate an upper confidence \(U_t(a)\) such that, with high probability, \(q_*(a)\le Q_t(a)+U_t(a)\). Then select \[A_{t+1}=\operatorname*{argmax}_a\big[Q_t(a)+U_t(a)\big]\]

  • small \(N_t(a)\): large \(U_t(a)\), the estimate is uncertain
  • large \(N_t(a)\): small \(U_t(a)\), the estimate is accurate

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.7

Silver illustrates this with three belief distributions over action values: the arm with the widest distribution may have the highest plausible value even if its mean is not the highest. After picking it we are less uncertain about its value and more likely to pick another arm, until we home in on the best arm. Either the optimistic arm really is good (and choosing it costs little regret), or choosing it shrinks its bonus quickly; both outcomes are useful. The bonus is in reward units. Unlike ε-greedy, the rule does not keep spending a fixed fraction of pulls on an arm that is well measured and clearly worse: because the bonus grows like √(ln t) for a fixed count, such an arm is still revisited, but only about ln t times in t steps, rarely rather than never. That is where logarithmic regret comes from.

Hoeffding's Inequality and UCB1

Hoeffding's inequality

i.i.d. \(X_i\in[0,1]\), sample mean \(\bar X_n\):

\[\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbb{E}[X]>\bar X_n+u\big]\le e^{-2nu^2}\]

Solve for the bonus

Set \(e^{-2N_t(a)U_t(a)^2}=t^{-4}\):

\[U_t(a)=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N_t(a)}}\]

UCB1: select each arm once, then \[A_{t+1}=\operatorname*{argmax}_a\Big[Q_t(a)+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N_t(a)}}\Big]\]

Sources: Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer (2002); David Silver, Lecture 9

Apply Hoeffding to the rewards of arm a: P[q*(a) > Q_t(a) + U] ≤ exp(−2N_t(a)U²). Choose the failure probability p and solve exp(−2NU²) = p, giving U = √(−ln p / 2N). Shrinking p over time, here p = t^(−4), makes the bonus grow slowly with t, so every arm is eventually re-examined; solving gives √(2 ln t / N). Here t is the number of pulls so far and the rule chooses pull t + 1, exactly as in Auer et al. The derivation is motivation, not proof: N_t(a) is random and depends on the data, which the full regret analysis has to handle. The theorem assumes independent, stationary rewards in [0, 1]. The index can exceed 1 even for Bernoulli arms; it is an optimistic index, not a clipped probability. Untried arms are selected explicitly rather than dividing by zero.

UCB1 Regret Bound

Theorem (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer). For independent rewards in \([0,1]\), after any number of steps \(t\), \[\mathbb{E}[L_t]\ \le\ 8\ln t\sum_{a:\Delta_a>0}\frac{1}{\Delta_a}\ +\ \Big(1+\frac{\pi^2}{3}\Big)\sum_a\Delta_a\]

  • logarithmic in \(t\): the order of the lower bound
  • small gaps are hard to tell apart, so the bound grows as \(\Delta_a\) shrinks
  • an expectation over runs, not a promise for one run
  • often loose in practice

Sources: Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer (2002), Theorem 1; Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

This is the classical finite-time UCB1 bound from Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer, written with gaps; π is the number 3.14…, not a policy. The inverse-gap sum excludes optimal arms. Silver's slide abbreviates the theorem with Δ_a in the numerator; the primary paper has 1/Δ_a, as shown here. Small gaps make the bound large because near-equal arms are hard to distinguish, although each such mistake costs little. For the three arms of the demonstrations (gaps 0.30 and 0.15) the first term is 8 ln t × (3.33 + 6.67) = 80 ln t, about 456 at t = 300, far above the regret of 17.70 observed at t = 300 on the previous slide: the bound is valid but loose. Gap-free analyses give a √(mt ln t)-type bound that stays useful when gaps are tiny.

Bayesian Bandits

Bayesian Bandits

  • use a prior \(p[\mathcal{R}]\) and the history \(h_t=A_1,R_1,\dots,A_t,R_t\)
  • Bernoulli arm: \(R\in\{0,1\}\) with \(\mathbb{P}[R=1]=\theta_a=q_*(a)\)

With prior \(\theta_a\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), after \(s\) successes and \(f\) failures from arm \(a\): \[\theta_a\mid h_t\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+f),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\theta_a\mid h_t]=\frac{\alpha+s}{\alpha+\beta+s+f}\]

  • \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)\) is uniform on \([0,1]\); \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are shapes, not step sizes

Sources: Russo et al. (2018), A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling; David Silver, Lecture 9

The likelihood of s successes and f failures is θ^s (1 − θ)^f. Multiplying by the Beta prior density, proportional to θ^(α−1) (1 − θ)^(β−1), simply adds s and f to the exponents: the posterior is again Beta (conjugacy). The posterior mean includes the prior counts; with a uniform prior and no data it is 1/2. α and β here are shape parameters, not the step size α of earlier lectures. Silver's Bernoulli-bandit slide labels α as the count of zero rewards; we use the standard convention in which α grows with successes. Uncertainty about θ is epistemic and shrinks with data; a single Bernoulli reward stays random even when θ is known exactly. Independence across arms is an assumption of these examples, not a requirement of Bayesian methods.

Thompson Sampling

Probability matching: select each action with the probability that it is optimal, \[\pi(a\mid h_t)=\mathbb{P}\big[q_*(a)>q_*(a'),\ \forall a'\ne a\mid h_t\big]\]

Thompson sampling implements it:

  1. sample \(\tilde\theta_a\sim p(\theta_a\mid h_t)\) for every arm
  2. select \(A_{t+1}=\operatorname{argmax}_a\tilde\theta_a\)
  3. observe \(R_{t+1}\) and update that arm's posterior

Uncertain arms are more likely to be the maximum: optimism without an explicit bonus.

Sources: Russo et al. (2018), A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling; David Silver, Lecture 9

Probability matching can be hard to compute analytically; Thompson sampling sidesteps the computation with one posterior sample per arm per step. Because the posteriors are continuous, ties have probability zero, so the arm with the largest sample is chosen with exactly its posterior probability of being best. That probability is not the arm's posterior mean, nor its chance of a successful reward. The sampled values are plausible mean parameters, not synthetic rewards. Silver notes that Thompson sampling attains the Lai–Robbins lower bound; for Bernoulli arms this has been proved, and the Russo et al. tutorial surveys such results and their limits. Like UCB, the exploration is driven by uncertainty, but no confidence level or bonus constant is chosen.

Beta samples are drawn exactly as a ratio of Gamma variates.

Quick Check: Posterior Mean

An arm has posterior \(\mathrm{Beta}(8,2)\). What does its posterior mean 0.8 tell us?

Three different quantities: an arm's mean, our uncertainty about it, and the probability that it is the best arm.

Sources: Russo et al. (2018), A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling

Answer: the predictive probability of reward 1 is 0.8. For the Beta–Bernoulli model, integrating θ under Beta(8, 2) gives 8/(8 + 2). The reward itself remains 0 or 1. Selection probability is a joint comparison: an arm with mean 0.8 is rarely selected against a posterior concentrated near 0.99, and almost always against poor alternatives. Students sometimes read a Thompson sample as reward noise; the sampling is over uncertainty about the model parameters. Whether the posterior is calibrated depends on the prior and likelihood being appropriate.

Information State Search

Value of Information

Safe arm: known mean 0.6. Unknown arm: mean 0 or 1, equally likely; one pull reveals which. \(H\) pulls remain.

StrategyExpected total reward\(H=1\)\(H=2\)
always pull the safe arm\(0.6H\)0.61.2
probe once, then pull the better arm\(0.5+0.8\,(H-1)\)0.51.3

After the probe each pull is worth \(\tfrac12\times1+\tfrac12\times0.6=0.8\), so probing pays when \(H>1.5\). A probe cost \(c\) moves this to \(H>1.5+5c\).

Information is worth more when there is more time left to use it.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation

Two hidden worlds: the unknown arm always fails or always succeeds, each with prior probability 1/2. The probe's expected immediate reward is 0.5 and it reveals the world exactly. Afterwards the best mean is 0.6 or 1, so each later pull is worth 0.5 × 0.6 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.8 in expectation. Always safe gives 0.6H; probing first gives 0.5 + 0.8(H − 1). With H = 1 safe wins, 0.6 against 0.5: the probe gives up 0.1 now and there is no later pull to use what it reveals. With H = 2 probing wins, 1.3 against 1.2. If the probe also costs c, probing is strictly better when 0.5 − c + 0.8(H − 1) > 0.6H, that is H > 1.5 + 5c: from H = 2 at c = 0, H = 5 at c = 0.5 (H = 4 ties at 2.4) and H = 7 at c = 1. Equivalently, the most worth paying for the probe is 0.2H − 0.3, which grows with the horizon. Silver: the value of information is how much a decision-maker would pay for it before deciding, and it is higher when there is more time to use it. Perfect revelation is deliberate; a general Bernoulli arm is not known after one pull, so this formula is not a general value-of-information estimator. Ask the class for the answer at H = 1 before showing the table.

Information State Space

  • information state \(\tilde s_t=f(h_t)\): a summary of all information so far
  • Bernoulli arms: the Beta shapes \((\alpha_a,\beta_a)\) of every arm
  • each action and reward moves \(\tilde s\) to \(\tilde s'\): an MDP over information states

With \(h\) steps remaining:

\[V_h(\tilde s)=\max_a\ \mathbb{E}_{r\mid\tilde s,a}\big[r+V_{h-1}(\tilde s')\big]\] \[V_0(\tilde s)=0\]

The second term values what the action teaches.

Solving this MDP is Bayes-optimal for the assumed prior (Bayes-adaptive RL; Gittins indices), but exact solution is usually intractable.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Russo et al. (2018); Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

The bandit has no changing external state, but the learner's posterior changes as data arrive, so the Bellman equation can be written over beliefs and the remaining horizon. The expectation averages over predictive outcomes under the current belief; different actions reveal different information and lead to different next beliefs. When h = 1 the future term vanishes and the best action is the one with the largest predictive immediate reward. With a longer horizon the value of information can change the choice, as on the previous slide. For independent Beta–Bernoulli arms the shape parameters are sufficient statistics. Gittins indices solve the discounted, infinite-horizon version arm by arm; simulation-based search (Guez et al.) approximates larger problems. Bayes-optimality is relative to the prior and likelihood, not a guarantee for every fixed environment, and the number of information states grows quickly with arms and horizon.

Beyond Stationary Bandits

Non-Stationary Rewards

If \(q_*(a)\) drifts, old rewards mislead. A constant step size \(\alpha\) forgets them geometrically:

\[Q_{n+1}=Q_n+\alpha\,(R_n-Q_n)=(1-\alpha)^n Q_1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{n-i}R_i\]

Example: an arm's mean jumps from 0.2 to 0.8 after 100 rewards. Twenty rewards later:

EstimateWeight on the 100 old rewardsExpected estimate
sample mean, \(\alpha=1/n\)\(100/120=0.83\)0.30
constant \(\alpha=0.1\)\(0.9^{20}=0.12\)0.73
The price of tracking is noise: with \(\alpha=0.1\) the estimate keeps a standard deviation of about 0.09 when \(q_*=0.8\).

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.5

This is Sutton and Barto's exponential recency-weighted average: reward R_i gets weight α(1 − α)^(n−i), and the weights plus (1 − α)^n sum to 1. The example uses expected values, so it can be checked by hand. After 100 rewards with mean 0.2 and 20 with mean 0.8, the sample mean still gives the old regime weight 100/120 and has expectation (100 × 0.2 + 20 × 0.8)/120 = 0.30. With α = 0.1 the old rewards keep total weight 0.9^20 = 0.12 (the initial value is negligible by then), so the expectation is 0.8 − 0.6 × 0.9^20 = 0.73. After 100 rewards at 0.8 the sample mean expects 0.50 and the constant-α estimate 0.80. The price: a constant step size never averages the noise away. In steady state its variance is α q(1 − q)/(2 − α): 0.1 × 0.16/1.9 = 0.0084, a standard deviation of 0.09 at q = 0.8, whereas the sample mean's shrinks like 1/√n. Larger α tracks faster and is noisier. A changing bandit also needs continued exploration of arms that once looked poor; changing the estimator alone does not solve that, and the stationary UCB1 bound does not cover this setting.

Contextual Bandits

  • Bandit: select an arm
  • Contextual bandit: observe a context \(S_t\), then select \(A_t\)
  • MDP: \(A_t\) also changes the next state

Linear model with features \(\mathbf{x}(s,a)\):

\[\hat q(s,a,\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{x}(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}\]

LinUCB adds a bonus for the uncertainty in \(\mathbf{w}\).

Which situation needs an MDP model of future consequences?

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Sutton & Barto (2018), §2.9; Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

Answer: the route. A contextual bandit observes information about the current decision before acting and optimises the immediate reward given that context; contexts arrive from a distribution the action does not influence. The advert example fits only under that modelling assumption: real advertising can change future user behaviour and would then need a richer sequential model. Features x(s, a) share data between related decisions, using the same linear notation as the function approximation lecture. LinUCB estimates w by least squares and adds c√(xᵀA⁻¹x), so its bonus reflects uncertainty in feature directions rather than raw arm counts. Do not infer the problem class from the application's name; ask what the action changes and which future consequences enter the objective.

Deep Exploration

A corridor needs \(L\) consecutive right moves; any left move ends the attempt.

Exploration\(\mathbb{P}[\text{reach the end}]\)\(L=6,\ p=\tfrac12\)
dithering: right with probability \(p\) at every step\(p^L\)\(\tfrac{1}{64}\approx0.016\)
committed: “all right” for the whole attempt, with probability \(p\)\(p\)\(0.5\)

On average 64 attempts per success against 2. The gap grows exponentially with \(L\).

Explore with plans that stay consistent for a whole episode: e.g. sample a plausible model and follow its optimal policy.

Sources: Russo et al. (2018), A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling

Dithering exploration, such as ε-greedy, decides afresh at every step, so reaching a reward that needs a long specific sequence has probability p^L: with L = 6 and p = 1/2 that is 1/64 ≈ 0.016, and the number of attempts until the first success is geometric with mean 64. Choosing once per attempt between "all right" and something else, with probability 1/2 each, succeeds with probability 1/2, a mean of 2 attempts. At L = 10 the comparison is 1/1024 against 1/2. The comparison changes temporal dependence; it is not a learning algorithm. Commitment works here because "all right" is one of two coherent plans; it does not find a rewarding sequence in every MDP. The example motivates temporally extended exploration, such as sampling a plausible model and following its optimal policy for a whole episode (posterior sampling, next slide), not persistence with an arbitrary bad policy. In the literature this is called deep exploration (Osband, Van Roy and colleagues).

Exploration in MDPs

The same principles apply, with uncertainty carried through the dynamics.

Optimism

  • model-free: initialise \(Q(s,a)\) to \(R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\), an upper bound on \(q_*\) when \(R\le R_{\max}\) and \(\gamma<1\)
  • model-based: unknown transitions lead to a maximal-reward state (R-max)

Posterior sampling

  • sample an MDP from \(p[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R}\mid h_t]\), solve it, follow its policy for an episode
  • Bayes-adaptive MDP: plan over \(\langle s,\tilde s\rangle\)
Uncertainty about an action's immediate reward is only part of the uncertainty about its long-term value.

Sources: David Silver, Lecture 9: Exploration and Exploitation; Russo et al. (2018); Lattimore & Szepesvári (2020)

R_max/(1 − γ) is the geometric-series bound on any discounted return when every reward is at most R_max and γ < 1; it is not a sensible unqualified initialiser for average-reward or undiscounted continuing tasks. Run any model-free method (Monte Carlo control, Sarsa, Q-learning) from it and unvisited state–action pairs look attractive. R-max (Brafman and Tennenholtz) builds an optimistic model in which unknown transitions lead to a maximally rewarding state, then plans in it. Optimism and posterior sampling make exploration valuable several transitions before new evidence arrives. Following one sampled MDP's policy for an episode gives the temporal consistency of the corridor example. Exact Bayesian planning reasons over the physical state and the information state together, which is generally far more expensive. UCB in MDPs must also account for uncertainty from policy improvement, not only from evaluating the current policy. None of this transfers a bandit regret bound unchanged to deep RL.

Exploring with a Learned Model

Dyna Architecture

Two kinds of experience

  • real: act in the environment and see what happens
  • simulated: ask the learned model \(\hat{\mathcal M}\) of Lecture 8 what would happen

Example: the agent moves right and lands one cell over, reward 0. Dyna stores this in its model, then replays it many times while planning.

Each real step

  1. act and observe a transition
  2. direct RL: update values
  3. model learning: update \(\hat{\mathcal M}\)
  4. planning: update values again from simulated transitions

Dyna: learn a model from real experience; learn and plan values from both real and simulated experience.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.2; David Silver, Lecture 8

Dyna is the integrated architecture of Silver's Lecture 8 (Integrating Learning and Planning). It sits in this lecture because it needs both halves of the course so far: a learned model (Lecture 8) and exploration (this lecture). Real experience samples the true dynamics, S′ ∼ P(· | s, a) with reward R(s, a); simulated experience samples the learned model, S′ ∼ P̂(· | s, a) with reward R̂(s, a). Model-free RL uses only real experience; pure sample-based planning uses only simulated experience. Dyna uses both. The same value update is applied to both kinds of transition, so planning gives each real transition extra work. Reusing a wrong model can reinforce a wrong answer, which is why direct RL from real data stays in the loop, and, as the end of this section shows, why exploration still matters once the model looks complete.

Dyna-Q Algorithm

For each real step from state \(S\):

  1. \(A\leftarrow\varepsilon\)-greedy\((S,Q)\); take \(A\), observe \(R,S'\)
  2. \(Q(S,A)\leftarrow Q(S,A)+\alpha\big[R+\gamma\max_a Q(S',a)-Q(S,A)\big]\)
  3. \(\text{Model}(S,A)\leftarrow R,S'\) (deterministic world)
  4. repeat \(n\) times: pick an observed \((S,A)\), set \(R,S'\leftarrow\text{Model}(S,A)\), apply update 2
  5. \(S\leftarrow S'\)
\(n\) planning updates per real step: computation is traded for real experience.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.2; David Silver, Lecture 8

Tabular Dyna-Q: step 2 is direct RL (Q-learning), step 3 is model learning, step 4 is planning. Planning samples state–action pairs uniformly from those already observed; with n = 0 this is plain Q-learning. Uniform replay wastes updates on pairs whose successor values have not changed: on a stored path A → B → C → end with rewards 0, 0, 1, γ = 0.9 and α = 1, replaying C, B, A backwards reaches the exact values 1, 0.9, 0.81 in three backups, while forward sweeps A, B, C need seven. Prioritised sweeping (Sutton and Barto §8.4) backs up the predecessors of recently changed values first. The next slide runs this algorithm; the last three slides of this section ask what happens when the world changes and the model goes stale.

When the Model Is Wrong

The world changes after the model has learned it. Two cases:

ChangeWhat the agent doesIs the model corrected?

A model is only corrected where the agent goes. Exploration has to send it where the model might be out of date.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.3, Examples 8.2 and 8.3

Sutton and Barto's blocking maze (Example 8.2) and shortcut maze (Example 8.3). When the environment gets worse, the out-of-date model leads the agent straight into the change, so ordinary experience corrects it, after a spell of poor performance. When the environment gets better, nothing in ordinary experience reveals it: the old route still works, the planned values stay consistent with the model, and ε-greedy exploration rarely makes the long, specific detour needed to find the opening (the dithering problem of the Deep Exploration slide). This is the model-based version of the non-stationary bandit: old data, here stored in the model, misleads, and the agent must keep testing actions that once looked poor. Ask the class which case they expect to be harder before revealing the last column.

Dyna-Q+

While planning, give each modelled transition a small bonus that grows with the time since it was last tried for real:

\[\text{simulated reward}=r+\kappa\sqrt{\tau}\]

\(\tau\): steps since the agent last took this action in this state. Actions never tried in a visited state are modelled as “stay put, reward 0”, so they earn the bonus too.

Example, \(\kappa=0.001\), goal reward 1:

Not tried forBonus
100 steps0.01
10,000 steps0.1
Planning carries the bonus back along whole routes, so the agent sets off on purpose to re-test a distant corner.

Sources: Sutton & Barto (2018), §8.3

Dyna-Q+ (Sutton and Barto §8.3) changes only the planning step: a simulated transition from (s, a) uses the reward r + κ√τ, where r is the modelled reward and τ the number of real time steps since (s, a) was last tried. Worked: κ√100 = 0.001 × 10 = 0.01 and κ√10000 = 0.001 × 100 = 0.1, so with goal reward 1 a pair untested for 10,000 steps looks like a tenth of a goal. Sutton and Barto also let planning use actions never tried from a visited state, modelled as leading back to the same state with reward 0 (their footnote); the demo does the same, which is a second difference from plain Dyna-Q besides the bonus. Compare UCB1: its bonus shrinks as an action is tried more often; Dyna-Q+'s grows the longer an action goes untried, a recency bonus suited to a world that may change. Because planning backs the bonus up through the model, the agent forms a multi-step plan to reach the stale pair: the temporally consistent exploration the Deep Exploration slide asked for. κ must be small next to the real rewards, or the agent explores for ever (next slide, κ = 0.01).

Exercise (5 mins): Three Quick Checks

  1. Means \((0.4,\ 0.7)\). You pull the first arm ten times. What is the regret?
  2. Prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)\); you observe 3 successes and 1 failure. What is the posterior mean?
  3. One pull remains. Is information for later worth anything?

In pairs: explain the mechanism before naming an algorithm.

Answers
  1. \(10\times(0.7-0.4)=3\), whatever the ten outcomes were
  2. \(\mathrm{Beta}(4,2)\), with mean \(\tfrac{2}{3}\)
  3. No: there is no later decision to use it, so maximise the expected reward of the last pull

Use as an exit check, ideally before opening the answers. The regret answer does not depend on whether the ten outcomes were lucky, because regret is a sum of gaps. The posterior update adds three successes and one failure to the prior shapes, giving Beta(4, 2) with mean 4/6 = 2/3. With one pull left, the continuation term in the information-state Bellman equation is zero, so information has no instrumental value under a pure reward objective; a scientific objective that values knowledge itself would be a different problem. Finish by asking which assumptions (stationarity, context, effect of actions on future states) students would check before borrowing a method for an application.

Each principle was developed in the bandit setting, and the same principles carry over to MDPs (Silver's conclusion). The methods differ in what drives exploration: random coverage that ignores uncertainty; an initial optimism that is soon forgotten; a confidence bonus that shrinks with counts; posterior samples that explore in proportion to the chance of being best; and explicit planning over what will be learnt. Dyna-Q+ adds a recency bonus inside planning, so that a learned model is re-tested where it may have gone stale. Before ending, ask learners to name the mechanism behind one method in their own words, and one assumption it relies on.
